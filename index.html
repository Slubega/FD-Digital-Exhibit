About the exhibit

Who gets to write the record?

Most of what the slave system wrote down about Black people was written for buying and selling. This exhibit follows Frederick Douglass as he moves from being written about to doing the writing.

The author

Frederick Douglass (c. 1818–1895)

Douglass was born enslaved in Talbot County, Maryland. He escaped to the North in 1838 and became one of the most famous antislavery speakers in the country. In 1845 he published his Narrative. The book made him famous and it also made him easy to find. It named the people who had owned him, and Hugh Auld was angry enough to buy Douglass from his brother Thomas so he could recapture him.

Douglass left for Britain and Ireland that same year. He spent almost two years lecturing there while his freedom was still unsettled.

The text

The Heroic Slave (1853)

The Heroic Slave is the only fiction Douglass ever published. It first appeared in Autographs for Freedom, a gift book from the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society edited by Julia Griffiths. The book was sold to raise money for the antislavery cause.

The story is based on Madison Washington, a real man who led a revolt on the slave ship Creole in November 1841. The ship was carrying enslaved people from Virginia to be sold in New Orleans. The rebels forced it to sail to Nassau in the Bahamas, where British law let most of them go free.

How to read this exhibit

The pages move in order. The Record shows Douglass as a line in a legal transaction. The Story shows him giving another enslaved man a history. The Image shows him arguing that anyone can own their own picture. Each page ends with a question for you to sit with.

Archival item 1: the record

Bill of Sale & Deed of Manumission

Before Douglass could be legally free, he had to be legally sold one more time.

Date
November 30 – December 12, 1846
Parties
Thomas Auld, Hugh Auld, Walter Lowrie (agent), Anna & Ellen Richardson
Price
£150 sterling ($711.66)
Collection
Frederick Douglass Papers, Manuscript Division, Library of Congress
Series
General Correspondence, 1841–1912, box 3, reel 1

Context

How a free man was bought

By 1846 Douglass was a celebrity in Britain and still a fugitive in America. Hugh Auld had made it known that he wanted Douglass back. Two Quaker women from Newcastle, Anna and Ellen Richardson, decided to raise the money to buy his freedom.

Hugh Auld agreed to take £150. The Richardsons' agent asked for proof that Hugh actually owned Douglass. So on November 30 Thomas Auld signed a new bill of sale that transferred Douglass to his brother for $100. On December 5 Hugh filed a deed of manumission in Baltimore. On December 12 the papers changed hands along with a receipt for $711.66. Douglass was legally free.

The bill of sale names him "Frederick Baily alias DouglasHe was born Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey in Talbot County, Maryland, in 1818. He took the name Douglass in New Bedford after his escape. The clerks spelled both names their own way.," misspellings and all. It is a document about him that he had no part in writing.

The argument it started

Did buying him prove slavery right?

Some abolitionists were furious. Henry C. Wright wrote to Douglass and called the papers "Bill of Sale of your body and soulHenry C. Wright, a Garrisonian abolitionist, wrote this to Douglass on December 12, 1846. He argued that paying Auld admitted a slaveholder could own a person. Douglass answered him ten days later.Wright to Douglass, December 12, 1846." To him, paying Auld meant admitting Auld had something to sell.

Douglass answered that his critics were mixing up two different acts.

"…the crime of buying men into slavery, with the meritorious act of buying men out of slavery."

Douglass to Henry C. Wright, December 22, 1846

Douglass took the deal because he wanted to go home to his wife and children without being kidnapped. Still, the argument never fully went away. His freedom came through a sale, and white people's money and signatures made it official.

Connection to The Heroic Slave

Living in the chattel records

The first page of The Heroic Slave complains that Virginia remembers its great white men and forgets Madison Washington. Douglass says Madison

"lives now only in the chattel recordsThe clerk who filed Douglass's deed of manumission wrote that it was recorded in "one of the Chattel Records of Baltimore County." Douglass uses the same phrase for Madison Washington.Deed of manumission, Library of Congress, mss11879.03002, image 96 of his native State."

The Heroic Slave, Part I

He adds that Madison holds no higher place in those records than a horse or an oxThe full line from Part I: Madison "holds now no higher place in the records of that grand old Commonwealth than is held by a horse or an ox."The Heroic Slave, Part I. This bill of sale is exactly that kind of record. It lists Douglass the way it would list livestock, with a price and a buyer. When Douglass wrote that line in 1853, he was describing a document he had held in his own hands.

The novella also rewrites his own rescue. In the story a white Ohioan named Listwell helps Madison, just like the Richardsons helped Douglass. But Listwell only slips him some files. Madison cuts his own chains and takes the ship himself. Douglass gives his hero the kind of freedom that no one had to sign for.

Question to consider

If a person's freedom has to be paid for and signed by someone else, whose freedom is it? Why might Douglass have wanted to tell a story where nobody pays?

The text, 1853

The Heroic Slave

Douglass's only novella sits between the two archival items. It is where he stops being the subject of the record and starts writing one for somebody else.

  1. I

    The Forest. In Virginia, Listwell overhears Madison alone in the woods, swearing to be free. Listwell leaves a changed man.

  2. II

    The Fugitive. Five years later Madison turns up at Listwell's home in Ohio. Listwell helps him reach Canada.

  3. III

    The Coffle. Madison goes back for his wife and is recaptured. Listwell sees him chained for sale and secretly passes him files.

  4. IV

    The Creole. A white sailor, Tom Grant, tells how Madison led the revolt and sailed the ship to freedom in Nassau.

Thread 1

The record versus the hero

Douglass opens by admitting how little history kept about Madison. That is why the story is fiction. Where the archive gives him a ledger, Douglass invents speeches, thoughts, and a love for his wife. The novella is a replacement record. It is the history Virginia refused to write.

Thread 2

The white witness

We only see Madison through white men. Listwell watches him in the forest and Tom Grant reports the revolt. This matches the bill of sale in an uncomfortable way. Douglass's freedom only counted once white men signed for it, and Madison's heroism only reaches the reader through white men who saw it. Douglass may be showing his white readers how they need to be taught to see a Black hero.

Thread 3

Freedom and money, again

The story was first printed in a book sold to raise money for abolition. Douglass's freedom was bought. His story about a man who could not be bought was also sold to fund the fightAutographs for Freedom was edited by Julia Griffiths for the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society, which raised money for the cause and for Douglass's newspaper.Autographs for Freedom, 1853. Money follows freedom all the way through this exhibit.

Thread 4

A picture in words

Douglass spends a long time describing what Madison looks like. He gives him a strong, dignified body and a face that commands respect. That careful portrait leads straight to the next item, where Douglass explains why pictures of Black people matter.

Archival item 2: the image

"Pictures and Progress"

Fifteen years after a bill of sale described him, Douglass wrote a lecture about the power of describing yourself.

Date
Delivered December 3, 1861, Boston (manuscript undated)
Author
Frederick Douglass, in his own hand
Type
Lecture manuscript
Collection
Frederick Douglass Papers, Manuscript Division, Library of Congress
Series
Speech, Article, and Book File, 1846–1894

Context

The most photographed man in America

Douglass was one of the most photographed Americans of the nineteenth century. He sat for portraits again and again, and he always looked serious and dignified. He knew that most pictures of Black people in America were cartoons and minstrel images drawn by white artists.

In December 1861, early in the Civil War, he gave a lecture in Boston that newspapers called "Pictures and Progress." He argued that making pictures is part of what makes us human.

"man is everywhere, a picture making animal."

"Pictures and Progress," 1861

He praised photography for making portraits cheap. A poor servant girl could now own a better likeness of herself than a queen once could. The second half of the lecture turns to the war and argues the Union has to end slavery to survive.

Why the manuscript matters

The handwriting is the point

Item 01 is written in a clerk's hand about Douglass. Item 02 is written in Douglass's own hand. You can see him crossing out words and revising. The first document turns him into a price. The second shows him thinking. Putting them side by side shows how far he moved in fifteen years, from a name on a bill of sale to the author of his own image.

Connection to The Heroic Slave

Drawing a hero

"Pictures and Progress" explains what Douglass was already doing in The Heroic Slave. When Listwell first sees Madison, Douglass slows the story down to paint him. He describes his build, his face, and the way he carries himself. White readers in 1853 had seen plenty of images that made Black men look foolish. Douglass gives them a portrait of a Black man as noble as any founding father.

The lecture also answers the bill of sale. The servant girl who owns her picture owns something about herself. In 1846 Douglass was the thing being owned. By 1861 he was arguing that every person, rich or poor, Black or white, should control how they are seen.

Question to consider

Is writing a hero like Madison Washington a kind of picture-making? What does a people gain when they control their own image?

Reflection

Why these items, and why this design

Text size

Suleman Lubega
ENGL 054: African American Literature to 1940
Dr. Sabrina Evans-Rangel
Rough draft, November 2026

I started with the first page of The Heroic Slave. Douglass says Madison Washington lives only in the chattel records of Virginia. That line stuck with me because Douglass was once in those records too. So I went looking for the record that had his name in it. The Library of Congress has copies of the bill of sale and deed of manumission from 1846. Those papers are the reason I picked this topic. They show a famous writer and speaker being handled like property only seven years before he wrote a story about a man who refused to be handled that way.

I picked "Pictures and Progress" as my second item because I wanted the exhibit to end with Douglass in control. The bill of sale was written about him. The lecture is in his own handwriting. In it he argues that people should own their own image, and that connected back to how carefully he describes Madison in the novella. Together the two items give the exhibit a direction. It moves from the record, to the story, to the image.

That direction is why I built the site as separate pages in a fixed order. The Garnet exhibit we looked at in class used tabs, and I liked how each tab let one item have its own space. I added a page for the novella in the middle because it is the bridge between the two documents. Each item page ends with a question so the reader has to make the connection too.

I chose a dark design on purpose. Records like the bill of sale were meant to sit in a drawer and never be looked at again. I wanted each document to look like it was being pulled into a spotlight. The gold color is for money and the dark red is for the price, because money keeps showing up across all three pieces. Douglass was bought, his novella was sold to fund abolition, and his lecture talks about portraits becoming cheap enough for anyone. I also used a typewriter-style font for the facts about each item so they look like a ledger. That way the facts look like the kind of record Douglass was fighting against, and his own words appear in a larger, more personal font.

The hardest decision was how to talk about the Richardsons and Listwell. I did not want to make the white helpers look like villains. They did help. But I think Douglass was uncomfortable that his freedom needed their money and signatures, and the novella feels like his answer to that. In the story the white ally only passes along the tools. Madison does the freeing. Building this exhibit changed how I read The Heroic Slave. I now see it as Douglass writing the record that the archive left out.
